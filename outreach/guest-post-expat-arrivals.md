## Proposed title
How Foreigners Actually Rent an Apartment in Tokyo (2026)

## Article

Renting your first apartment in Tokyo is rarely the problem newcomers expect it to be. Most arrive worried about price. What actually trips them up is the guarantor system, the upfront cash, and how fast good units disappear. I have spent the last few years living in Tokyo and running furnished rentals here, so I see the same avoidable mistakes on repeat. Here is what genuinely works.

### The guarantor barrier, and the three ways around it

Most standard Japanese leases ask for a guarantor: someone with local income who agrees to cover your rent if you default. A foreigner who just landed usually cannot provide one. This is the single biggest reason applications get rejected, and it catches people off guard because nothing about it is obvious from the listing.

There are three realistic paths around it:

1. **Share houses.** A furnished private room with shared common areas, no guarantor and no key money, move in within days on a passport and visa. Rent typically runs 40,000 to 90,000 JPY a month, often with utilities included. It is the easiest first home.
2. **Monthly mansions.** A private furnished studio with its own kitchen and bathroom, again no guarantor, usable as a real address for admin. Expect roughly 90,000 to 200,000 JPY a month. Good for privacy without a year-long commitment.
3. **Standard rentals via a guarantor company.** For an unfurnished long-term lease, most agencies now accept a paid guarantor company (hosho gaisha) instead of a personal guarantor, for a fee of around half a month of rent. This unlocks the normal market once you have a residence card and some proof of income.

### The upfront cost is the real surprise

The sticker rent is only part of the story on a standard lease. Between deposit, key money, agency fee, the first month, and the guarantor company fee, move-in costs on an unfurnished apartment often land near four to five months of rent paid at once. Share houses and monthly mansions exist largely because they collapse that number to one month or less. If your budget is tight in the first few months, that difference matters far more than the headline rent.

### Good apartments go in 24 to 48 hours

Tokyo is not a market where you browse for weeks. Desirable units listed on the major rental platforms are often gone within a day or two. The practical consequence: if you are relocating, do a focused scouting window four to six weeks before your start date, not the week you arrive. Line up your documents first (passport, visa or certificate of eligibility, and proof of funds) so you can move the moment you see the right place.

### What to verify before you sign

- **Renewal and cleaning fees.** Ask what you owe at renewal and at move-out. A fixed cleaning fee written into the contract is normal; vague "restoration" charges beyond ordinary wear are not, and normal wear is meant to be the landlord's cost.
- **What the rent actually includes.** Utilities, internet, and management fees vary a lot, especially in share houses and monthly units.
- **The real commute.** A cheaper ward often just means more distance from a fast line. Weigh rent against minutes on the train, not against the map.

### A note on getting help

You can absolutely do this alone, and plenty of people do. Where a guide helps is compressing the learning curve: knowing which segment fits your situation, having your paperwork ready, and not losing the good unit while you hesitate. Whether you use one or not, the same principle holds: understand the guarantor question before you start, and you remove ninety percent of the friction.

Tokyo rewards preparation. Sort the guarantor path and the timing, and the rest tends to fall into place.
