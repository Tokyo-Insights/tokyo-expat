## Proposed title
Why Japanese Landlords Reject Foreign Tenants (and How to Get Approved Anyway)

## Article

If you have applied for an apartment in Japan and been turned down without a clear reason, you are not doing anything wrong. Rejection of foreign applicants is common, and it usually has little to do with you personally. I live in Tokyo and operate rentals here, so I see both sides of the table. Once you understand what a Japanese landlord is actually worried about, the whole process stops feeling like a wall and starts feeling like a checklist.

### It is about perceived risk, not prejudice

A landlord in Japan is rarely trying to discriminate for its own sake. They are managing a single, illiquid asset and trying to avoid three specific outcomes: unpaid rent, trouble communicating during a problem, and a tenant who leaves the country suddenly with unpaid bills or damage. A foreign applicant with no local credit history, limited Japanese, and a visa that might expire reads, on paper, as uncertainty on all three. Everything that follows is the landlord trying to reduce that uncertainty.

That reframing matters, because it tells you exactly what to fix. You are not trying to prove you are a good person. You are trying to remove each specific worry.

### The five things that actually get you approved

1. **A guarantor company (hosho gaisha).** This is the single biggest lever. A paid guarantor company agrees to cover your rent if you default, which removes the landlord's main fear for a fee of around half a month of rent. Most agencies work with one and can enroll you. If an application stalls, this is usually the missing piece.
2. **Proof of stable income or funds.** A contract of employment, recent payslips, or sufficient savings shown on a bank statement all reduce the payment worry. If you have not started work yet, savings do a lot of the same job.
3. **A residence card and a longer visa.** The more time left on your status of residence, the less the "might disappear" concern weighs. If your visa is short, lean harder on the other points.
4. **A reachable point of contact.** Landlords relax when they believe a problem can be communicated and solved. A bilingual contact, or an agency that will mediate, quietly removes the language worry.
5. **A clean, complete application, fast.** Good units move in a day or two. Turning up with your documents already assembled signals reliability and beats other applicants to it.

### The segments where rejection barely happens

If you need a home now and cannot yet assemble all of the above, aim at the part of the market built for exactly this situation. Share houses and furnished monthly units accept a passport and a visa, skip the guarantor entirely, and let you move in within days. They cost more per square metre than a standard lease, but they buy you time to build the local footprint (a residence card, a bank account, an income record) that makes the standard market say yes later.

### The mistake that costs people weeks

The most expensive error I see is applying blind to standard listings, getting rejected a few times, and concluding the market is closed. It is not closed. It is gated, and the gate has a known key. Either enroll a guarantor company and present income, or start in the foreigner-friendly segment and graduate up. Both work. What does not work is repeating the same unassisted application and hoping for a different answer.

Get the guarantor question sorted before you fall in love with a specific apartment, and most of the rejection disappears.
